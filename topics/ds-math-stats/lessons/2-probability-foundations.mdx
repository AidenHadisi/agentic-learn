# Probability Foundations

Lesson 1 taught you how to *summarize a fixed list of numbers*. Real work is messier: the numbers haven't arrived yet, a test result just came back, or you need a fair price for a risky bet. Probability is the language for that uncertainty — how to update beliefs when evidence arrives, and how to summarize a *chance process* the way you summarized a dataset.

We'll stay concrete. Counts before formulas. Every identity earns its keep with a worked example.

## Events and Conditional Probability

An **experiment** produces one **outcome** from a **sample space** $S$ (every possibility). An **event** is a subset of $S$ — a collection of outcomes you care about. If you roll a die, $S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and "even" is the event $\{2,4,6\}$.

Often you learn something that rules outcomes out. A positive medical test. The word "FREE" in an email. Knowing the first card was a heart. That information doesn't create a new kind of probability — it **restricts the sample space** and **renormalizes** what's left so the remaining mass still sums to 1.

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad (P(B) > 0)$$

Read it in plain English: *of the probability that lives in $B$, what fraction also lives in $A$?* There is no event called "$A \mid B$". The bar marks *evidence*, not a third set.[¹]

<Stepper>
<Step>Imagine a fair deck. Draw one card. $P(\text{Ace}) = 4/52 = 1/13$.</Step>
<Step>Now you're told the card is a **heart**. Throw away the other 39 cards. The restricted world has 13 hearts, including 1 ace.</Step>
<Step>$P(\text{Ace} \mid \text{Heart}) = 1/13$. Same as unconditional ace probability here — hearts and aces "don't interfere."</Step>
<Step>Contrast: $P(\text{Heart} \mid \text{Ace}) = 1/4$. Same joint (1 ace of hearts), **different denominator**. Conditioning on aces vs conditioning on hearts are different worlds.</Step>
</Stepper>

That last contrast is the whole game. $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ share a numerator but divide by different totals. Swapping them is the **prosecutor's fallacy** — treating "how likely is this evidence if guilty?" as "how likely is guilt given this evidence?"

Rearranging the definition gives the **multiplication rule**:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B)$$

Story: probability of both = probability of the condition × probability of the other *given* the condition.

### Independence

Two events are **independent** when learning one doesn't change the probability of the other:

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A) \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$

Independence is *not* "can't happen together." That is **mutually exclusive** ($A \cap B = \emptyset$). If two events with positive probability are mutually exclusive, knowing one occurred *forces* the other to probability 0 — they are dependent.

<Callout variant="key" title="Conditioning is restriction">
$P(A \mid B)$ means: discard everything outside $B$, then ask what fraction of the survivors is in $A$. Always ask "restricted to *which* world?" before you compute.
</Callout>

## Bayes' Theorem

Bayes is not a new law of nature. It is the definition of conditional probability, flipped to answer the question you actually care about.

You often know $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{hypothesis})$ — how a test behaves *if* someone is sick, how a word behaves *if* an email is spam. You want $P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{evidence})$. Same joint, different denominator:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\, P(H)}{P(E)}$$

$P(H)$ is the **prior** (base rate). $P(E \mid H)$ is the **likelihood**. $P(H \mid E)$ is the **posterior**. The denominator $P(E)$ accounts for *every* way the evidence can arise — including false alarms.[²]

Formulas alone hide the trap. Work in **natural frequencies** — counts from one population that keep the base rate intact.[³]

### Worked example: a screening test

Disease prevalence is 1%. The test is 99% sensitive ($P(+ \mid \text{disease}) = 0.99$) with a 5% false-positive rate ($P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05$). You test positive. What's $P(\text{disease} \mid +)$?

Most people's gut says "about 95%" or "99%." Let's count.

<Stepper>
<Step>Imagine **10,000** people. Diseased: $1\%$ → **100**. Healthy: **9,900**.</Step>
<Step>Of 100 diseased: $99\%$ test + → **99** true positives.</Step>
<Step>Of 9,900 healthy: $5\%$ test + → **495** false positives.</Step>
<Step>Total positives: $99 + 495 = 594$. Of those, only 99 are sick.</Step>
<Step>$P(\text{disease} \mid +) = 99/594 \approx 0.167$ — about **17%**, not 99%.</Step>
</Stepper>

Why so low? Almost everyone is healthy, so even a small false-positive rate produces a *crowd* of healthy positives that swamp the true cases. The test accuracy numbers $P(+ \mid \text{disease})$ and $P(+ \mid \text{healthy})$ are *not* what a positive patient needs. They need the posterior, which mixes those numbers with prevalence.

Same arithmetic as Bayes:

$$P(\text{disease} \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$$

<Callout variant="warn" title="Base-rate fallacy">
Ignoring prevalence and equating "test accuracy" with "chance I have it" is one of the most reliable errors in human reasoning — clinicians make it too.[⁴] Always ask: *of the people who look like this, what fraction actually are?*
</Callout>

### Bayes in data science (conceptually)

A classifier is the same move. For each class $c$, score $P(c) \times P(\text{features} \mid c)$, then normalize across classes to get $P(c \mid \text{features})$. Class frequencies in training are priors; rare classes (fraud, defects, rare diseases) are base-rate problems wearing different clothes. High recall on a rare class still yields low precision unless false positives are tiny.

## Random Variables, Expectation, and Variance

Conditional probability updates *events*. Often the outcome you care about is a **number**: dollars won, number of clicks, latency in ms. A **random variable** $X$ assigns a number to each outcome.

For a discrete $X$, the **probability mass function** (PMF) $p(x) = P(X = x)$ is the distribution's histogram with bar heights equal to probabilities — the same role frequencies $1/n$ played for a fixed dataset.

### Expected value

The **expected value** reweights each possible value by its probability:

$$E[X] = \sum_x x\, P(X = x)$$

This is the balance point of the PMF — the same "center of mass" idea as the mean, but the weights are probabilities instead of $1/n$.[⁵] It is a property of the *chance process*, not of one sample. It need not be a value $X$ can actually take.

**Fair die.** $X \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, each with probability $1/6$:

$$E[X] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = 3.5$$

Never lands on 3.5. Expectation is not "the most likely value" (that's the mode) and not "what usually happens."

**Fair game intuition.** If $G$ is your net gain from a bet, the game is **fair** when $E[G] = 0$. Casinos set payouts so $E[G] < 0$ for the player. Expectation is the long-run average stake — not a promise about the next roll.

### Variance of a random variable

Spread for a random variable mirrors population variance from Lesson 1, with a known center $\mu = E[X]$:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2$$

Standard deviation $\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$ restores the original units. The computational form $E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$ is usually easier — and it reminds you that $E[X^2] \neq (E[X])^2$ unless the variance is zero.

Continuing the die:

$$E[X^2] = \frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6} = \frac{91}{6}$$

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{91}{6} - \left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.92, \quad \mathrm{SD}(X) \approx 1.71$$

| Fixed dataset (Lesson 1) | Random variable (now) |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$ | $E[X] = \sum x\, P(X=x)$ |
| $s^2$ uses $n-1$ because $\bar{x}$ is estimated | $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ uses known $\mu = E[X]$ |
| Summarizes *your sample* | Summarizes the *chance process* |

### Linearity of expectation

The most useful property in applied probability:

$$E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$$

always — **even when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent**. Independence is *not* required. (Contrast: variances add only under independence / zero correlation. $Y = -X$ makes $X+Y$ constant, so $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = 0$, not $2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)$.)

**Indicator trick.** Let $I = 1$ if event $A$ occurs and $0$ otherwise. Then $E[I] = P(A)$. Counting becomes summing indicators; expectation becomes summing probabilities.

**Matching hats.** $n$ people get randomly shuffled hats. Let $X$ be the number who get their own hat. The distribution of $X$ is messy, but write $X = I_1 + \cdots + I_n$ where $I_j = 1$ if person $j$ matches. Person $j$'s hat is equally likely to be any of the $n$, so $E[I_j] = 1/n$. The $I_j$ are *dependent* (if $n-1$ match, the last must), yet:

$$E[X] = \sum_{j=1}^{n} E[I_j] = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$$

for every $n \ge 1$. Brute force for $n=3$ (six permutations) also averages to 1 — linearity gives the same answer without listing anything.[⁶]

<Callout variant="tip" title="Bridge from Lesson 1">
Descriptive stats: reweight each *observed* number by $1/n$. Expectation: reweight each *possible* number by its probability. Same balance-point intuition; different weights.
</Callout>

## Closing

You now have two moves that cover most of probabilistic reasoning in data work:

1. **Condition and renormalize** — evidence restricts the world; Bayes flips likelihoods into posteriors and refuses to ignore base rates.
2. **Summarize the chance process** — $E[X]$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ are the mean and variance of a distribution, with linearity of expectation as a workhorse that survives dependence.

Next we'll meet the named distributions that arise over and over — not as a zoo to memorize, but as generative stories with fingerprints you'll recognize in data.
