# Probability Foundations

Descriptive statistics gave you a toolkit for data you already have. You can take a pile of numbers and say what's typical, how much it varies, and which direction it leans. Every one of those tools is backward-looking: it summarizes what already happened.

But most questions worth asking point forward. Your dashboard says 40% of last quarter's incidents were latency-related — that's a fact about the past. The question you actually care about is "we're deploying in ten minutes, how worried should I be?" That's a question about something that hasn't happened. Probability is the language for those questions, and it's the foundation everything later in this course is built on.

This chapter builds that language from the ground up. We'll pin down what a probability even is, learn the handful of rules it must obey, and then spend most of our time on the single idea that does the heavy lifting in data science: what happens to a probability when you learn something new.

## The Experiment and the Sample Space

Before you can talk about how likely something is, you have to be precise about what "everything that could happen" means. That sounds pedantic. It isn't — most probability mistakes in the wild are really mistakes about this step.

An **experiment** is any process with an uncertain result: rolling a die, shipping a deploy, showing a user a checkout page. The **sample space** is the complete list of possible outcomes of that experiment, written so that exactly one of them will happen. An **event** is any collection of outcomes you care about.

A useful picture, borrowed from Harvard's probability course, is a tray of pebbles. Each pebble is one outcome. Each pebble has some weight, and the weights of all pebbles add to 1. An event is you scooping up a handful of pebbles, and the probability of that event is the total weight in your hand. Running the experiment is reaching in and drawing one pebble.

The two requirements on that list are that the outcomes **cannot overlap** and that they **cover everything**. For a single die roll, "the result is 1 or 3" and "the result is 1 or 4" cannot both be outcomes — they share the possibility of a 1, so they'd double-count it. The atomic outcomes are 1 through 6, and everything else is built from them.

What the list should contain is a genuine modeling decision, not a fact you look up. For a deploy you might use just two outcomes, succeeded and failed. Or you might need four: succeeded, failed on tests, failed on latency, failed on rollback. Neither is more correct in the abstract. The right granularity is the coarsest one that can still express every question you plan to ask.

<Callout variant="warn" title="The most common setup error">
Choosing a sample space too coarse to express your question. If your outcomes are only "succeeded" and "failed," you cannot ask anything about *why* it failed — that information was thrown away before you started, and no amount of arithmetic will recover it.
</Callout>

## Two Ways to Read a Probability

When you say an event has probability 0.30, you could mean one of two quite different things, and it's worth knowing which one you mean.

The **frequency** reading says: if this experiment were repeated over and over under the same conditions, the event would occur about 30% of the time. This is the natural reading for anything genuinely repeatable — cache hit rates, checkout conversion, incidents per deploy. It's also the reading that connects directly to data, because you can estimate it by counting.

The **belief** reading says: 0.30 is how confident I am, and I'd accept a bet at those odds. This is what you mean when you say "there's a 70% chance the latency spike is DNS." That outage happens once. There is no long run to average over. But you still need a number to make a decision with.

Here is the important part: the mathematics is identical either way. Every rule in this chapter applies to both readings without modification. The interpretation changes what the number *means* and how you'd justify it, not how it behaves. Both readings show up constantly in data science work, and the only real mistake is sliding between them mid-argument without noticing.

## The Rules

Probability is a way of spreading a fixed budget of certainty — exactly 1.0 of it — across the outcomes in your sample space. Three rules follow from taking that seriously.

First, **no probability is negative**. Negative weight makes no sense under the pebble picture, and if you'd accept a bet priced on a negative probability you'd be agreeing to lose money for certain.

Second, **the whole sample space has probability 1**. Something on the list will happen. If you doubt that, your list is incomplete, which is a modeling problem rather than an arithmetic one.

Third, **probabilities of non-overlapping events add**. If an incident can't be both Sev-1 and Sev-2, then the chance it's one or the other is just the sum. Non-overlapping piles of weight combine by adding.

Two useful consequences fall straight out. Since an event and its opposite together cover everything and don't overlap, their probabilities sum to 1, so $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. This is the **complement rule**, and it's often the fastest route to an answer: the probability that at least one of five services fails is painful to compute directly but easy as one minus the probability that all five stay up.

The other consequence handles overlap. If two events *can* both happen, adding their probabilities counts the overlap twice, so you subtract it back off:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Here $A \cup B$ means "A or B or both" and $A \cap B$ means "both A and B." If 40% of incidents are latency-related and 25% happen during a deploy window, the chance an incident is one or the other is *not* 65% unless those two never co-occur — and as we're about to see, they very much do.

## Reading a Two-Way Table

Almost everything in this chapter becomes concrete the moment you look at a table of counts, so let's build one and stay with it.

Suppose your team logged 200 production incidents last quarter. You classify each one two ways: did it happen during a deploy window, and was it latency-related?

| | Deploy window | Outside deploy window | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Latency-related** | 40 | 40 | 80 |
| **Not latency-related** | 10 | 110 | 120 |
| **Total** | 50 | 150 | 200 |

Write $L$ for "latency-related" and $D$ for "during a deploy window." This single table holds three different kinds of probability, and the only thing distinguishing them is **which number you divide by**.

A **joint** probability is about two things happening together. It's a cell divided by the grand total. The chance a randomly chosen incident is both latency-related and in a deploy window is

$$P(L \cap D) = \frac{40}{200} = 0.20$$

A **marginal** probability is about one thing, ignoring the other. It's a row or column total divided by the grand total — and it gets its name because you read it off the margins of the table. The chance an incident is latency-related, deploy window or not, is

$$P(L) = \frac{80}{200} = 0.40$$

and similarly $P(D) = 50/200 = 0.25$. Notice what happened to the other variable: you summed across it and it disappeared. That operation is called *marginalizing*, and it's the same move as collapsing a pivot table down to one dimension.

A **conditional** probability is about one thing *given* that you already know the other. This is where the denominator changes. Suppose you know the incident happened during a deploy window. You're no longer choosing from all 200 incidents — you're choosing from the 50 in that column. So

$$P(L \mid D) = \frac{40}{50} = 0.80$$

The notation $P(L \mid D)$ reads "the probability of L given D." Eighty percent of deploy-window incidents are latency problems.

Now flip it. Given that an incident was latency-related, how likely was it to have happened during a deploy? Now your world is the 80 incidents in the latency row:

$$P(D \mid L) = \frac{40}{80} = 0.50$$

<Callout variant="key" title="Same cell, different denominators">
$P(L \mid D) = 0.80$ and $P(D \mid L) = 0.50$ came from the *same 40 incidents*. They differ only in what population you divided by. Conditional probabilities are not symmetric, and treating them as if they were is probably the single most expensive mistake in applied statistics.
</Callout>

Say both out loud and the difference is obvious. "Among deploy-window incidents, 80% are latency problems." "Among latency problems, 50% happened during a deploy." Those are clearly different claims about different groups. It's only in symbols that they blur together.

## Conditioning Means Restricting, Then Renormalizing

Now let's name what we just did, because it generalizes past tables of counts.

When you learn that $B$ happened, two things occur. First you **restrict**: every outcome where $B$ didn't happen is off the table, gone, no longer possible. Second you **renormalize**: the surviving outcomes have to carry a total probability of 1 again, so you scale them all up by dividing by however much probability survived.

That's exactly what the definition says:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

The numerator is the probability that survived *and* is in $A$. The denominator is all the probability that survived. The division is the renormalization step. It's defined whenever $P(B) > 0$ — you can't condition on something impossible, since there'd be nothing left to renormalize.

Check it against the table. $P(L \cap D) = 0.20$ and $P(D) = 0.25$, so $P(L \mid D) = 0.20 / 0.25 = 0.80$. Same answer as counting within the column, which is reassuring — the formula is just bookkeeping for "count inside the restricted world."

A geometric version helps. Picture all possibilities as a square of area 1. The event $B$ is some region inside it. Learning $B$ happened means erasing everything outside that region and blowing up what's left to fill the whole square again. Areas that were small slivers of the original square can become large fractions of the new one. Nothing about the underlying world changed — only which part of it you're still considering.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Conditional probabilities are ordinary probabilities">
Once you've restricted to $B$, the function $P(\cdot \mid B)$ obeys every rule from earlier: nothing negative, the restricted world totals 1, non-overlapping events still add. Conditioning gives you a new, smaller probability model — not a different kind of object.
</Callout>

## Chaining Events Together

Rearranging the definition gives something useful in its own right. Multiply both sides by $P(B)$:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A \mid B)$$

This is the **multiplication rule**, and it's how you compute the probability of a sequence of things all going a particular way. Read it as a story: first $B$ happens, and then, *in the world where $B$ has happened*, $A$ happens too.

Consider a canary release that has to clear three gates in order. Unit tests pass 95% of the time. Given that they passed, the error-rate check passes 90% of the time. Given both of those passed, the latency check passes 85% of the time. What's the chance a release clears all three?

<Stepper>
<Step>Chance of clearing gate 1: $P(G_1) = 0.95$</Step>
<Step>Given gate 1 cleared, chance of clearing gate 2: $P(G_2 \mid G_1) = 0.90$. So the chance of clearing both is $0.95 \times 0.90 = 0.855$</Step>
<Step>Given both cleared, chance of clearing gate 3: $P(G_3 \mid G_1 \cap G_2) = 0.85$. Multiply again: $0.855 \times 0.85 = 0.727$</Step>
</Stepper>

So a bit under 73% of releases make it through cleanly, even though every individual gate looks reassuringly high. Multiplying numbers below 1 erodes them fast, which is worth internalizing: a pipeline of individually reliable steps is much less reliable than any of its steps.

Notice that each factor after the first is a *conditional* probability, given everything before it. That's what makes this correct even when the gates influence each other. A release that barely squeaked past the error-rate check probably isn't a healthy release, and its latency check is likelier to fail too. The conditional factors absorb that.

## Independence Is Not the Same as "Can't Both Happen"

Sometimes learning $B$ tells you nothing at all about $A$. When that happens we say $A$ and $B$ are **independent**, and it means precisely this:

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

Conditioning didn't move the number. Restricting the world to $B$ left $A$'s share of it unchanged. Feed that into the multiplication rule and you get the more familiar form:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

You are only allowed to multiply plain, unconditional probabilities when the events are independent. That's the whole content of the rule.

Test it on our incident table. If $L$ and $D$ were independent, the joint probability would have to be $P(L) \times P(D) = 0.40 \times 0.25 = 0.10$. The actual joint probability is 0.20 — twice as large. So they're dependent, and in the direction you'd expect: deploys make latency incidents more likely, not less.

Now the confusion this section exists to prevent. Two events are **mutually exclusive** (or disjoint) when they cannot both happen — an incident can't be both Sev-1 and Sev-2. Students routinely treat "mutually exclusive" and "independent" as roughly the same idea, or as opposites on some single scale. They are neither.

<Reveal prompt="If two events are mutually exclusive, are they independent?">
Almost never. Suppose $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive and both have nonzero probability. If you learn that $A$ happened, then $B$ definitely did *not* happen, so $P(B \mid A) = 0$. But $P(B)$ was some positive number. Conditioning changed it dramatically — from positive all the way to zero. That's the *opposite* of independence. Mutually exclusive events are maximally dependent: each one, if it occurs, completely rules the other out.
</Reveal>

The clean way to keep them apart is to notice they answer different questions. Mutual exclusivity is about whether the events can **co-occur** — it's a structural fact about the sample space, and it's what licenses you to add probabilities. Independence is about whether one event carries **information** about the other, and it's what licenses you to multiply them. A coin flip and a die roll are independent and can absolutely both happen. Sev-1 and Sev-2 can't both happen and are wildly dependent.

## Breaking a Problem into Cases

The last tool in this chapter handles a very common situation: you can't easily compute how often something happens overall, but you *can* compute how often it happens within each of several groups.

Say support tickets arrive through three channels. Half come from chat, 30% from email, 20% from phone. Escalation rates differ by channel: 10% of chat tickets get escalated, 25% of email tickets, and 40% of phone tickets. What fraction of all tickets get escalated?

The trick that makes this easy — and it's a trick worth using constantly — is to stop working in percentages and imagine a concrete population instead. Take 1,000 tickets and follow them through.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR
  R[1000 tickets] --> C[Chat: 500]
  R --> E[Email: 300]
  R --> P[Phone: 200]
  C --> C1[Escalated: 50]
  C --> C2[Not escalated: 450]
  E --> E1[Escalated: 75]
  E --> E2[Not escalated: 225]
  P --> P1[Escalated: 80]
  P --> P2[Not escalated: 120]
`} />

<Stepper>
<Step>Split 1,000 tickets by channel: 500 chat, 300 email, 200 phone</Step>
<Step>Apply each channel's escalation rate: $500 \times 0.10 = 50$, $300 \times 0.25 = 75$, $200 \times 0.40 = 80$</Step>
<Step>Total escalated: $50 + 75 + 80 = 205$, so $P(\text{escalated}) = 205/1000 = 0.205$</Step>
</Stepper>

About 20.5% of tickets get escalated. Written in symbols, what you just did is the **law of total probability**:

$$P(E) = P(E \mid C)P(C) + P(E \mid M)P(M) + P(E \mid H)P(H)$$

with $C$, $M$, and $H$ for chat, email, and phone. Each term is one path down the tree: the chance of landing in that group, times the chance of escalating once you're there. Add up every path that ends in escalation.

The requirement is that your groups form a **partition** — every ticket belongs to exactly one channel, and the channels cover all tickets. That's the same no-overlap-and-cover-everything condition we needed for the sample space, applied one level down.

It's worth seeing this for what it is: a weighted average. The overall escalation rate of 20.5% sits between the lowest channel rate (10%) and the highest (40%), pulled toward chat because chat has the most volume. Any time you've computed an overall rate from segment rates weighted by segment size, you've used this law without naming it.

<Callout variant="key" title="Why the count version is easier">
The 1,000-ticket walkthrough and the formula are the same arithmetic. But almost everyone finds the counts easier, because the group sizes stay visible the whole way through. In percentage form it's easy to lose track of the fact that chat is a much bigger group than phone. Whenever a probability problem feels slippery, try re-imagining it as a concrete population of 1,000 and counting.
</Callout>

## Putting It Together

Everything in this chapter came from one idea: probability is a fixed budget of 1.0 spread over a well-specified list of possibilities. The rules about adding, complementing, and overlapping are just careful bookkeeping on that budget.

The part that will keep paying off is conditioning. Learning something new means throwing away the possibilities that are now ruled out and rescaling what remains, which is why the denominator in a conditional probability is the conditioning event rather than the whole world. Everything else followed from that: the multiplication rule is conditioning rearranged, independence is the special case where conditioning changes nothing, and the law of total probability is what you get by conditioning on each of several cases and adding the results back up.

Carry one thing forward above all: $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different numbers. Our incident table put them at 0.80 and 0.50. Keeping those straight — always asking "given what? among which group?" — is most of what separates careful probabilistic reasoning from the confident, wrong kind.
